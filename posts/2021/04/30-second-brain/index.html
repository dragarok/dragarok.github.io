30 second brain

  • Human Brain is a fascinating topic and this book covers basics on human brain using very small concepts.
  • Each neuron will have at most one axon. Axons typically split into separate branches before connecting with dendrites of other neurons.
  • Grey matter: Called grey because of lack of myelination that makes other parts of brain seem white.
  • Hippocampus: Formation and Consolidation of Memories, Spatial Navigation.
  • Myelin coating: Insulates the axon from other nearby axons and dramatically increase the speed of nerve impulse / spikes traveling along.
  • Parietal Cortex: Experience of space and position and heavily involved in attentional processes.
  • Purkinje Cells:
    • Found excessively in cerebellum
    • One of the largest neurons in the brain
    • Elaborately branching dendritic structures.
    • Long-range inhibitory control over output parts of cerebellum, enabling fine motor co-ordination and error correction.
  • Brain Impulse:
    • 0.1 Volt
    • Lasts one- to two-thousandths of a second
    • Runs along axons at up to 480 kph (300 mph).
    • When meets synapse, triggers release of neurotransmitters.
  • Glial Cells:
    • 50 times more in number than neurons.
    • Help neurons wire together in developing brains
    • Help nurture neurons in adult brain
    • Insulate axons
    • Mop up dead cells
    • Recycle used neurotransmitters
    • Protect brain from infection
  • Neurotransmitters:
    • Small molecules to Medium Compounds to Giant Molecules : Peptides.
    • Stored in tiny spheres called synaptic vesicles.
    • Impulse releases them into synaptic gaps.
    • Many don’t trigger fast excitation or inhibition, but initiate quite slow metabolic processes in neurons, causing lasting changes in the strength of synaptic connections.
    • Can turn ON and OFF of important genes.
    • Nitric Oxide(NO) , a poisonous gas, is a most unlikely transmitter.
      • Cannot be stored in vesicles.
      • Released as it is produced within specialized active neurons.
      • Spreads into envelopes of brain, where it can affect many receptive neurons without the transmitting neuron having to be directly connected to them. Non-synaptic Signaling.
  • As needs change, different genes are turned on or off. This changing pattern of active genes is particularly notable in functioning of synapses.
  • Signals are dispatched from synapses to their central nuclei and there the DNA is ordered to turn on the required genes.
  • While genes certainly affect brain function, the fact that they can be influenced by the environment frees our behavior from rigid genetic determinism.
  • Cajal realized that Golgi’s images showed nervous system as a network of discrete cellular components. This allowed neurons to be studied as functional units of brain.
  • Brain Stem is the metencephalon, thalamus is diencephalon, and outer cortex is telencephalon.
  • Cerebellum:
    • Densely packed with cells
    • 10% of brain volume
    • Contains half the neurons found in entire central nervous system.
    • FUN FACT: We are unable to tickle ourselves due to cerebellum.
    • Forward Models : Predictions about sensory consequences of actions and cancel them out.
    • Inverse Models : Calculating movements necessary to achieve a desired action.
  • Brain Development:
    • Hollow tube formed from the skin of very early embryo.
    • Cells multiply more rapidly at the front of the tube, which enlarges, becoming the embryonic brain.
    • Newly produced cells transform into immature neurons.
    • By the time embryo is about 4 weeks old, they migrate to their destination, growing dendrites and axons and forming the first of what will be trillions of synaptic connections.
    • About half the embryonic connections are killed off. The ones that survive get myelinated.
    • Grey matter voluem increases gradually through childhood, peaks in early adolescence and shrinks as an adolescent becomes an adult.
    • Genomes don’t have all the recipe to make brain on their own: need to exploit information from the environment which helps fine tune the neuronal networks.
  • Different Animal Brains:
    • Fruit Fly Lacks cortex but has large antennal lobes and mushroom bodies dedicated to processing smell.
    • Rat has large areas of cortex devoted to processing information from its whiskers.
    • Fish has enlarged cerebellum for movement.
  • Humans are believed to have massive expansion of their brain due to bipedalism, larger social groups and the emergence of language.
  • Classic Conditioning:
    • Environment Stimulus.
    • Associated with a outcome.
    • Leads to new behavior with respect to the stimulus.
  • Instrumental Conditioning:
    • Association between action and outcome leading to increased propensity to do the action.
  • Connectionism:
    • Theoretical approach that emphasizes how interconnected network of nerusn can learn via simple local rules applied between pair of neurons.
  • Hypersynchronous state: Levels of neuronal synchrony exceed normal limits, so that large parts of brain start switching on and off together.
  • Phrenology: Individual variation in mental capabilities and personality characteristics can be inferred on the basis of differences in the shape of the skull.
  • Predictive Coding: Hermann von Helmholtz.
    • Perception as a form of inference.
  • Re-entrant connectivity : Neural signals flow in both direction between two areas.
  • Cognitive functions are neither fully localized nor wholly distributed in the brain.
  • Brain doesn’t have one language but many. Even worse, there may be a different language at different level: Neuronal, Ensemble and the whole brain.
  • Rate code vs Timing Code: How do distant groups of neurons co-ordinate their activity?
  • Alpha Waves: Hans Berger, inventor of EEG in 1920s.
    • Slow oscillations at about 10 Hz.
    • Predominantly across the back of the brain and more pronounced during relaxed wakefulness with the eyes closed.
  • Delta Waves: 1-4 Hz.
  • Theta Waves: 4-8 Hz.
  • Beta Waves: 12 - 25 Hz
  • Gamma Waves: 25 - 70 Hz and higher.
  • These are not well established categories though.
  • Epileptic Seizures: When neural oscillations get too strong, the brain can enter a state of hypersynchrony similar to brainstorm.
  • Proposals of Theory of Neuronal Group Selection:
    • Early brain development generates a highly diverse population of neuronal circuits.
    • There is a selection among these groups. Those which are used survive and strengthen.
    • Re-entry: Constant interchange of signals between widely separated neuronal populations.
  • Helmholtz and Perception:
    • What’s out there? question must involve brain inferring the external causes of its sensory signals. This means brain something similar to Bayesian Inference that describes how beliefs are updated as new evidence comes in.
    • The incoming sensory data is combined with ‘prior beliefs’ to determine the best probable cause, which correspond to perception.
    • Then these prediction errors are used to update prior beliefs.
  • Double Mode Network:
    • Group of brain region whose activity is reduced during the performance of externally directed task, and is more active in states of wakeful rest, mind-wandering, introspection or inwardly directed attention.
    • Includes medial parts of prefrontal and temporal lobes and posterior cingulate cortex.
  • Diffusion Tensor Imaging:
    • Depends on the fact that water molecules diffuse preferentially along axons, instead of across them.
  • Electroencephalography:
    • Detecting tiny variations in electric field at the surface of the brain, which are produced by the activity of population of neurons in the underlying cortex.
  • fMRI extends MRI to measure brain activity and is based on measuring the differences in blood oxygenation that go along with neural activity. Good spatial resolution but poor time resolution compared to EEG.
  • Some believe that revealing the network structure of the human brain might yield fewer clues than promised. One species with complete connectome maped is Nematode worm, Caenorhabditis elegans. Yet it’s behavioral features remain a mystery.
  • Scientists have used virus to smuggle an algal light-sensitive gene into rat neurons. When exposed to blue light, the neurons fired. Soon an ‘off’ as well as ‘on’ switch was found.
  • Wim Vanduffel et. al modified a region of frontal lobes in two monkeys to enable widespread light-induced activation. With blue light turning this region on, the monkeys were faster at object-tracking task. Optogenetics thus acts a great promise.
  • Montreal Method : Operate on patients to excise the parts of the brain from which epileptic seizures originate. Under local anaesthetic and asked questions to patients what kind of sensations they have when probing different parts of the brain. The ‘homunculus’ produced with colleague Herbert Jasper, is still in use today.
  • Without a task to perform, brain is almost as active as normal, but a default set of regions lights up instead of task related ones.
  • Default mode network : Importance of this network comes from it’s most promising putative role, that is daydreaming, which has been linked to both higher insight and creativity.
  • Hemispheres of Brain:
    • Characterizing left hemisphere as cold, logical and right as emotional and creative is an over-simplification.
    • Instead of tasks being delegated to one side or the another, both typically apply a different processing style to the same tasks. Left hemisphere is dominant for language, while the right has language functions such as recognizing intonation.
  • On experimenting with electrical probe to simulate neurons, it was discovered that simulation of a single neuron in the temporal lobes would reactivate entire memories in the patient.
  • TMS : Non-invasive procedure in which a brief magnetic pulse on the scalp simulates underlying cortical region.
  • Autoscopic experience: Experience of seeing one’s body from an external perspective.
  • Comatose state: Patients in such state show no signs of wakefulness or awareness. The brain activity during such states is similar to that observed during general anaesthesia.
  • Prefrontal Parietal Network: Frequently associated with consciousness, especially in so-called global workspace theories.
  • Qualia : Philosophical term broadly referring to the intrinsic properties of conscious experience.
  • Causality of EEG Signals : A slowly rising electrical brain signal seems to precede voluntary decisions to perform actions.
  • Supplementary Motor Area : Part of the frontal lobes and a possible neural source of the readiness potential. Direct simulation of this region produces the experiences of wanting to make a movement.
  • Daniel Dennett : Consciousness in terms of function that it supports and not by the raw essence of experience.
  • Binocular Rivalry : One image [ e.g. house ] shown to one eye and another image [ face ] shown to another eye. Brain cannot resolve this ambiguous input into single image, one’s conscious experience alternates betwen the house and the face.
  • Phantom Limb Syndrome : Brain builds a body model that doesn’t always match the physical body.
  • Global Workspace Theory : Specific mental contents such as perceptions, thoughts, intentions to act etc. remain unconscious unless or until they gain access to a global workspace, which broadcasts their contents across the brain, making them available for the flexible control of behavior.
  • Thalamic switch Theory : GAs turn consciousness off by reducing activity in specific parts of thalamus.
  • V4 : Samir Zeki in 1970s and has been closely associated with perception of color, although not limited to it.
  • Change Blindness : A phenomenon in which surprisingly large changes in visual scene go unnoticed if there is a gap between the images of if there are other more salient changes accompanying the transition.
    • A bed of roses will result in different mixture of wavelength during the dawn and the day but we will perceive the roses as a particular shade of red in both cases.
  • Super Colliculus : A pair of small structures beneath the cerebral cortex that play an important role in guiding fast visual reflexes, such as object tracking.
  • Some of the colors (such as magenta) don’t have corresponding wavelength - brain constructs it.
  • The key to understanding this comes from the fact that there is not just one route that connects the eyes to the brain but many ( around 10 in humans ), so damaging the brain might affect one route selectively.
    • Routes have evolved for different functions- one uses light to calibrate the biological clock and another is used to orient the eyes to sudden changes.
    • Fast seeing route ( via super colliculus ) that moves the eyes and a slow seeing route ( via the cortex ) that can detect the details of what is seen.
  • Synaesthesia : For small % of population, words may be colored, music may have a taste, or numbers may be visualized on a twisting, turning landscape.
  • Attention Mechanism: Since vision and rest of our senses are continually bombarded with information, brain has developed this mechanism to filter and prioritize ( such as amplifying its neural signal ) at the expense of other information.
  • Object Tracking:
    • Need to know the position of eyes in the socket.
    • Need to know position of the head relative to the body.
    • Signals from eye and neck muscles need to be linked to vision.
    • There appears to be separate pathways in the brain for locating and acting on an object versus knowing what the object is.
  • Sacks : The Man who mistook his wife for a hat, 24 essays reporting in from the extremes of the mind.
  • Alien movements : If our motor cortex becomes disconnected from the controlling regions, then movements are produced that are unexpected.
  • Medial Temporal Lobes : Related to long term memories.
  • Fragmentation of storage areas:
    • Semantic Memories [ Capital of France is] : Temporal Lobe
    • Memories from past events: Widely distributed across temporal cortex.
  • Broca’s area : Inferior Frontal Gyrus, is thought to play a central role in processing syntax, grammar and sentence structure.
  • Wernicke’s area: Superior Temporal Gyrus : Involved in comprehension of language.
  • Metacognition:
    • Think about thinking.
    • Not only important for monitoring how we learn new information, but also for communicating our subjective experiences with others.
    • Macaque monkeys, Dolphins and rats exhibit signs of metacognition when performing a perceptual or memory task. Others, such as pigeons don’t.
  • Negative emotional impact is twice as intense as the positive effect of gains, which affects our decision making in predictable ways.
  • Mirror Neurons : Activated during the execution of an action and by the sight of someone else performing that action.
  • Pheromones : Airborne chemical signals released by male mice that stimulate neurogenesis in the olfactory bulb and hippocampus in the brain of female mice.
  • Volume of Posterior Superior Temporal Sulcus pSTS predicted the level of loneliness in sample of people. It is linked to the processing of social cues.
  • Splitting the brain in half proved to alleviate the symptoms of epilepsy without apparent impairment.
  • Thought Insertions : Patients experiencing a lack of ownership of their own thoughts.
  • Schizophrenia : Perceptual hallucinations, false beliefs, thought insertions. Symptoms may arise from a failure to properly combine prior expectations with new sensory evidence.
    • Genes related to it : COMT, is involved in breaking down the neurotransmitter dopamine, involved in prediction-based learning.
  • Four meditation sessions were sufficient to increase working memory capacity. Five sessions showed increased performance on an attentional task that involved resolving conflict. Lessen sense of anxiety, anger and tiredness.
