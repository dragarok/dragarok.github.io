The Intelligent Investor

Graham, Benjamin;Jason Zweig

  • Obvious indications for growth in a business do not necessarily turn into obvious profits for investors.
  • Just because they are “experts” doesn’t mean they have dependable ways of selecting promising companies in promising industries.
  • Buy stocks as if they were groceries, not perfume! The really dreadful losses arose because the buyer forgot to ask, “How much is it worth?”
  • Have a simple portfolio:
    • High Grade Bonds
    • Diversified Leading common stocks.
  • Investment must be based on the “margin of safety” principle.
  • If an investor wants to participate in speculation, funds should be kept separate from those used for investing.
  • Investment: Promoting the safety of principal and an adequate return.
  • In collapses, common stocks for some writers became regarded as speculation. In improved markets, the speculations were termed as investments giving rise to the term reckless investor.
  • Although speculation is always fun, the author advises to limit it to what you can afford to lose, to take out your winnings if you should have any, and to never confuse speculations and investments.
  • Recommendations for defensive investors:
    • Buy shares only from well established investment brokers.
    • If funds are substantial, employ a recognized investment counselor.
    • Employ dollar cost averaging with the same fixed amount of money in the same stock at fixed intervals.
  • Recommendations for aggressive investors:
    • Search for undervalued and unrecognized companies with potential for improvement.
  • The investor should not expect better than a return of 8% on Dow Jones listed stocks, based on theory that it will fluctuate, investor should not expect uniform annual returns or growth; or should he be led astray by out-of-the-ordinary advances and declines.
  • The investor should not put all his funds into one basket. The investor who depends on the income from his portfolio must guard against the unexpected and minimize his risk.
  • Caution is advised with regard to believing that markets will never turn down again- a popular opinion after long periods of above-average stock performance.
  • In the century dated from 1871, total of 19 bear and bull cycles. Although there were major fluctuations until 1972, the annualized gain of value of stocks was 11%, dividends gave further value of 3.5% giving a total of 14% on average. All of this led some to believe that market could never turn down - a very dangerous view to hold.
  • In 1953, when it had advanced by 50% in 5 years, Graham advised caution but market defied his advice and went on to gain 100% in next 5 years getting to 584 in 1959. Market kept climbing although called “dangerous” by Graham and reached 685. Then it fell 27% in 1962, when IBM stocks fell by 50%, and those of less well known companies fell by 90%. This is caused by speculators believing themselves to be investors.
  • Anticipated rate of return is related to risk but it should be more related to the intelligent effort behind the investment decision.
  • Many maxims vs Graham:
    • Maxims: 50-50 bond and stocks.
    • Graham: 25-75 depending on market conditions. In bottom of bear, make stocks cover 75% with 25% bonds.
  • The truly conservative investor can at one side of cycle enjoy the benefits of rising values, and at the other side of cycle feel pleased about how little of his investment is declining in comparison with those of other, more aggressive, speculators.
  • Higher yielding bonds : Higher yields correspond to lower safety ratings by the agencies, and the possibility of default is always to be balanced against the desirability of higher income.
  • Beginner in investments : Don’t try to beat the market but focus on price vs value and to learn through experience, by investing small sums.
  • Less than top-quality securities should only be purchased at bargain prices.
  • The advice given by Graham is largely on the basis of what the enterprising investor should NOT DO. You better follow these guidelines:
    • Leave high-grade preferred stocks to the corporate buyers.
    • Avoid inferior bonds and preferred stocks, except at bargain purchase levels.
    • Avoid foreign government bonds, despite the high yields offered.
    • Be wary of new issues.
    • Avoid common stocks with excellent, but only recent, earnings.
    • The choice of purchase of bonds should be same as for the defensive investor.
  • Although many investors buy bonds in order to obtain an income and are tempted to buy inferior bonds because the income they offer is higher, it has historically proven unwise to follow. These are the bonds most likely to suspend payments when the market turns down, and most likely to lose face value.
  • It’s a bad business to accept the known possibility of a major loss of principal in exchange for 1-2% improvement in yearly income.
  • Second-grade bonds are liable, when the market turns down, to show a greater loss of face value than their better counterparts; however when the market recovers, most of these bonds will also recover. Investors who take advantage of this swing in value will do very well.
  • Newly issued shares may be issued on behalf of either an established company or a formerly private company now going public. Of necessity, the latter tend to be small operations with an inherent risk involved in buying their shares; they are launched when the market is on a upswing and may not survive a downswing.
  • Enterprising investor will:
    • Buy low and sell high.
    • Be careful with this selection. Buy extremely cheap picks.
    • Buy securities with special situations.
  • Graham doesn’t believe any formula exists for increasing the proportion of stocks held according to market behavior.
  • An examination of 45 funds specializing in growth stocks showed, that in one 10-year period they averaged a gain of 108%, while in the same decade S&P gained 105% and the narrower Dow-Jones gained 83%. During the following 2 years, the majority of growth stock funds did worse than both the S&P and DJIA.
  • To obtain better than average results, you must: Have a plan that has rules and it’s reasonably executed. Go against the crowd of other investors.
  • Bargain Issues: Stocks or bonds which, all facts considered, appear to be selling below their real value. They are thought to be a bargain only if the indicated value is at least 50% greater than the selling price.
  • For the investor, the three requirements are underlying safety, simplicity of choice and promise of satisfactory results.
  • Enterprising investor may look into purchase of anything he has adequate training on. He’ll avoid closing at full price foreign bonds, ordinary preferred stocks, secondary common stocks and original offerings, but may buy them at bargain prices: 2/3rd of appraised values.
  • Investor should solely observe, not react to the market price when already invested.
  • If you want to speculate, do so with your eyes open, knowing that you will probably lose money in the end. Be sure to limit the amount at risk; separate it completely from your investment program.
  • The intelligent investor will benefit from “pricing” whereas the investor who practices “timing” will become an speculator.
  • Dow Theory once known by all investors should have lost it’s value. Timing purchases with this theory is okay but trader who just stayed with a DJIA portfolio fared better than the ones who went in and out following the timing indications of the Dow Theory.

  • Recommends not to buy stock when the market valuation, the speculative premium, exceeds book value by more than a third.

  • Investor only looks for lows and highs. Meanwhile, if the investor has the available funds, he will look for the ever-present bargain opportunities.
  • Intelligent investor should follow: “Never buy a stock immediately after a substantial rise nor sell one immediately after a substantial drop.”
  • If it is important that the market value will not decrease, he should buy US savings bonds, series E or H. If, however, seeks the best interest rates, he should look for a selection of long-term corporate or tax-free municipal bonds, but must expect fluctuations in prices.
  • In average an individual stock investor performs worse than an investor who puts his money into a mutual fund.
  • The explanation for funds matching the index is that they are so large that they are essentially the market. Then current evidence points to the idea that most mutual funds perform worse than DJIA.
  • It’s always that “Bright, energetic, young people have promised to perform miracles with other’s people money since time began.”
  • Suggests it’s better to buy a closed-end fund at a 10-15% discount than an open-end fund with a 9% sales fee.
  • A balanced fund is one that has both stock and bond components. 23 different balanced funds showed that an average of 40% capital is placed in bonds. The fund’s average return on the offering price was 3.6% which was less than the return of US savings bonds during that period.
  • While brokers may have high integrity, their pay is based on commission sales, which can lead to advice that encourages investor to speculate.
  • If the investor must rely on the advice of other persons, he should limit himself strictly to conservative and unimaginative investments.
  • Some financial services produce highly technical reports on market activity and don’t give direct investment advice. Some make market forecasts, usually phrased or hedged cautiously that they can claim to have been right in their prediction no matter what actually happens. The intelligent investor may take notice of the information offered, and even of the suggestions, but will base his investment decisions based on a wide range of factors.
  • Although excuses can be few to many, it’s more likely that failures were caused by partners speculating on the market in their own account.
  • The institutional buyer of stocks in large quantities is as well informed as the broker he deals with. The small investor, in his ignorance and naivete is readily taken advantage of. He should listen to investment advice but bring it to his own judgment, or possibly even seek advice elsewhere.
  • The aggressive, enterprising investor will want the full details of the advice given, and will use his own judgment on whether to follow it. Not until his securities adviser has proved his value over a significant period of time will the investor accept his advice blindly.
  • For bond analysis, rule of thumb from Graham: EPS should be 3x the per-share fixed obligations of the company, among other recommendations.
  • For common stock analysis:
    • Capitalization and discount rate to compare and equate current price with an estimate of the future earnings.
    • For estimating capitalization rate, four key determinants are considered.
      • Operating and Financial results
      • Strong and weak points
      • Potential for future earnings
      • Comparisons with like companies in the same industry
  • Whenever calculus is brought in on calculations, it’s a warning signal that the operator was trying to substitute theory for experience, and usually also to give speculation the deceptive guise of investment.
  • Desirable securities:
    • Net income > Fixed Expenses * 3
    • EPS == 3 * Per-share fixed obligations
  • Common Stock Analysis requires comparing current price with an estimate of future warnings multiplied by an appropriate “capitalization factor”.
    • Cap rate is used to equate potential operating income to value.
    • Operating income: Difference between estimated revenues and estimated expenses of the present business model.
  • In addition to valuing current operating income, it accounts for a future income and earnings growth. It takes those earnings estimates and “discounts” the future cash flow back to the current value.
  • If earnings are expected to remain constant, a cap rate is probably the correct terminology; if earnings are expected to increase or decrease over time, discount rate is most likely appropriate term to use.
  • Factors affecting the capitalization rate:
    • General long term prospects: e.g. Chemical industries thought to outperform oil companies.
    • Management
    • Financial Strength and Capital Structure: e.g. Company with a heavy debt load.
    • Dividend Record
  • Value = Current(Normal) Earnings x (8.5 + 2 * Expected annual growth rate)
  • Graham applied this formula to a number of large companies. For two it proved valid; for four it did not- and he warns that forecasting the future does not have a high degree of reliability.
  • Two part Process:
    1. Appraisal on past performance, requires assumption that the world will remain unchanged for the next seven years.
    2. Determine what future conditions might alter this steady path.
  • Why investor should be cautious about EPS:
    • Can be manipulated both higher and lower than accounting laws.
    • An average earnings measure of 7-10 years is suggested for giving a more realistic reflection of earnings.
  • Don’t take a single year’s earnings seriously. If you do, watch for booby-traps in per-share figures.
  • Although primary EPS were reported to be $5.20, net income after special charges was $4.39, and fully diluted after special charges was $4.19. What seems might not always be the case.
  • Dilution factor : Allowance for the potential of conversion of bond issue into common shares.
  • Contingency Reserve: Profits put aside in order to even out future earnings.
  • Accounting rules:
    • Special Charges
    • Income tax benefits delayed.
    • Dilution Factor
  • Choice of Depreciation is the fourth one and fifth choice accountant has to make is whether R&D should be charged as they occur or amortized over several years. Sixth one is whether to value the inventory on the basis of FIFO or LIFO.
  • There are many maneuvers accountants employ, which to them are ethical, and to lawyers legal but to investors highly deceptive.
  • Although investor can use average growth rate for long-term figures, it is suggested to use an average when short-term growth has been strong.
  • Amortization is a fancy name for depreciation of an intangible asset.

Analyzing companies:

  • Although company can seem successful, high P/E ratio of around 60 can make decision to buy an such expensive pick extremely risky.
  • For the whole portfolio, the E/P ratio should be at least as high as the current high-grade bond rate.
  • If chosen from DJIA, it can contain small samples from each of the leading issues and the growth companies, or, with less effort, the investor could buy into an investment fund that is indexed to the Dow.
  • Exclude small companies; group as a whole is not suited for defensive investor though some small companies may be possible.
  • Current assets should be at least double the current liabilities.
  • Generally look for securities with uninterrupted payment for at least 20 years.
  • Current price should not exceed 15 times earnings averaged over previous years.
  • Current price should not exceed 150% of last reported book value, with allowance for a lower P/E but selling at higher proportion to book value. e.g. P/E of 9 with 250% of book value would be acceptable.

Which companies to exculde in the portfolio?

  • Too small companies.
  • Weak in financial state.
  • Have deficit stigma during the past 10 years.
  • Have insufficient history of dividend payments.

Among these, the one with weak financial state is the most important rule to look at. Use Google Stock Screeners for this purpose.

Very similar to an option. Difference: Warrants are issued and guaranteed by the company while options are not.
  • Convertible securities are not necessarily attractive than non-convertible ones. There is more security in convertible bond than in the stock, but the chances are the stock was overpriced when the bond was issued.

    • When not paid tax for too long, red flag should have been raised into the validity of their reported earnings.

  • Shareholders prefer a more liberal payout policy, while management prefers to retain the earnings inside the company.
  • Graham argues that dividends should either be paid out or shareholders should demand the profitability of retaining earnings to be proven.
  • Current practice is retention of a large part of the profits instead of the older practice of paying 75% of profit in dividends.
  • Previously, the market tended to favor companies with a liberal dividend policy: ones that pay most of their profits to shareholders.

  • Stock Split: Merely the restatement of common stock structure. Purpose is to reduce the market price to encourage wider purchase.
  • Proper Stock Dividend: Paid as representative of reinvested earnings in the recent past ( say two years or less). Valued amount of stock dividend should be transferred into the accounts from earned surplus to capital. Same effect as paying cash from earnings, only it’s not received by shareholder as cash; instead as more shares.
  • Stock dividend has tax advantages over a cash dividend.
  • Strong opinion: Companies need to have a written policy that specifies whether they will pay a certain portion of their earnings as a cash dividend or stock dividend.
  • The margin of safety of bonds can be calculated by comparing total value of business with it’s debts. If total value is 30 million and debt is 10 million, there is theoretical room for shrinkage of 2/3rds.
  • The difference between the % rate of earnings on stock at the price you pay for it and rate of interest on bonds, and that margin of safety is the difference which would absorb unsatisfactory developments.
  • In 1965, when book was written, the typical stock was selling at 11 times earnings, giving about 9% return as against 4% on bonds. In that case you had a margin of safety over 100%.
  • In 1970s, there is no difference between earnings rate on stock and bonds, there is no margin of safety here.
  • Margin of safety thus is dependent on the price paid. If entire group is overpriced, even diversified portfolio will not solve the problem, but wise individual selections might do.
  • Holding a single stock with margin of safety may or may not prove satisfactory, but holding several ones, each with a margin of safety, is more likely to do so by spreading the risk.
  • Broadest category of unconventional investments is undervalued common stocks of secondary companies, to be bought only when they are at less than 2/3rds of the indicated value. Another one is medium-grade corporate bonds when selling at depressed prices with considerable discount from their apparent value.
  • So, there are no good or bad stock , only cheap and expensive stock.
  • One lucky break may count for more than a lifetime of efforts. But one has to be ready, in position, and skilled in asset evaluation to recognize and “go for” the break.
