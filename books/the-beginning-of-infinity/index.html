Summary

Good Explanations

  • Deutsch attributes all progress, both theoretical and practical, to quest for good explanations.
  • Good explanations go far beyond extrapolations from experience of the creators.
  • Unique thing about humans is the ability to create good explanations. Everyrhing we do to improve our lives attributes to this ability.
  • The real source of our theories is conjecture, and the real source of our knowledge is conjecture alternating with criticism. We create theories by rearranging, combining,altering and adding to existing ideas with the intention of improving upon them. The role of experiment and observation is to choose between existing theories, not to be the source of new ones.
    • In fact this is the main thing about innovation in general: there is no rational definition of where to look for answers or good questions. And there is no bad theory as long as our main basis for it is criticism with proper logic.
  • They even go beyond biological knowledge which has limited reach. Another difference is that mutations are random; conjectures are intentional.
  • We have known the world both to be more bigger and violent, but also awesome in detail. And the more we get good explanations, the elegance increases as well.
  • Good explanations make it harder to even fool yourselves. Its hard to vary it specifically for cases it doesn’t work with. And since it has an autonomous meaning and autonomous domain of applicability.
    • e.g. It might touch in places where you don’t even want it to touch and if its a good explanation, it will work there effectively as well.
    • That’s what is up with autonomic nature of it.
  • Old ways of thought, which did not seek good explanations, permitted no process such as science for correcting errors and misconceptions. Improvements happened so rarely that most people never experienced one. Ideas were static for long periods. Being bad explanations, even the best of them typically had little reach and were therefore brittle and unreliable beyond, and often within, their traditional applications.
  • We need to understand the contradictory ideas together in one piece: we will never run out of problems but we will keep creating good explanations and improve on our ability to do so at an exponential pace too.

Fine Tuning Theory

  • Brandon Carter in 1974 calculated that if strength of interaction between charged particles were a few percent smaller, no planets or any huge objects would have formed. If it were a little greater, no stars would ever explode. So, no complex chemistry, and no life based on these assumptions.
    • The objection to this is that we are not sure if chemistry or planets are necessary to form life at all.
    • And that posits to the possibility of parallel universes where these values are instantiated differently as well.
  • Sciama says that if the value of these constants are close to optimal values for creating astrophysicists, then it would statistically refute anthropic explanation for the value. He concludes that universe is only just capable of producing astrophysicists when more constants are involved. because the amount of constants involved isn’t known.
    • That turns out to be a bad explanation because we don’t know the difference in same laws with different constants to different laws of physics. And there are infinitely many different possible laws of physics.
    • If laws of physics exist in one form with constants differing between parallel universes, then anthropic explanation should explain why different laws were not instantiated.
  • Before a discovery is made, no predictive process could reveal the content or the consequences of that discovery. So scientific discovery is profoundly unpredictable, despite the fact that it is determined by the laws of physics.

Mathematics

  • Seeking perfect system of mathematics without logical consistency is in a way form of justificationism. The object of mathematics is to understand and explain abstract entities. And it only has to be about good explanations, nothing more than that.
  • Finitism is also studied in maths.
  • Cantors paradox gives a good example on thinking about infinity.
  • The intuitive notion of proportion and Probability doesn’t work with infinities.
  • Then Godel’s incompleteness theorem cleaned it up about this space of infinity that we have based on “unprovable” theorems, “undecidable” problems.
  • There is nothing mathematically special about the undecidable questions, the non-computable functions, the unprovable propositions. They are distinguished by physics only. Different physical laws would make different things infinite, different things computable, different truths – both mathematical and scientific – knowable. It is only the laws of physics that determine which abstract entities and relationships are modelled by physical objects such as mathematicians’ brains, computers and sheets of paper.
  • Whether a mathematical proposition is true or not is indeed independent of physics. But the proof of such a proposition is a matter of physics only.
  • Mathematical truth is absolutely necessary and transcendent, but all knowledge is generated by physical processes, and its scope and limitations are conditioned by the laws of nature.
  • The whole motivation for seeking perfect foundation for Mathematics was mistaken. The object of mathematics is to understand abstract entities. Mathematics is there to seek good explanations too.
  • So there is something special – infinitely special, it seems – about the laws of physics as we actually find them, something exceptionally computation-friendly, prediction-friendly and explanation-friendly. The physicist Eugene Wigner called this ‘the unreasonable effectiveness of mathematics in the natural sciences’.
  • Classically computable problems don’t mean that there isn’t more.
  • Even unprovable mathematical explanations can be interesting and continue to be useful. And a lot of these explanations are not computable by classical computers but that’s us limited by Physics. Undecidability today no more contradicts the maxim that problems are soluble than does the fact that there are truths about the physical world that we shall never know.
  • Proof is a physical process: whether a mathematical proposition is provable or unprovable, decidable or undecidable, depends on the laws of physics, which determine which abstract entities and relationships are modelled by physical objects. Similarly, whether a task or pattern is simple or complex depends on what the laws of physics are.

Whenever a wide range of variant theories can account equally well for the phenomenon they are trying to explain, there is no reason to prefer one of them over the others, so advocating a particular one in preference to the others is irrational.
